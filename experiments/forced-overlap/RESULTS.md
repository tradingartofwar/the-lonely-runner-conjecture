## An overlap count that scales with the denominator

The tree bound follows Hunter's classical inequality, used for continuous-time Lonely Runner by Perarnau and Serra. For any tree whose vertices are signed classes and whose edges have weights $|B_c\cap B_e|$, a lower bound on safe numerators is
$$
\max\left(0,\ d-\sum_c|B_c|+\sum_{\{c,e\}\text{ in tree}} |B_c\cap B_e|\right).
$$
At a numerator with $m>0$ blocking classes, those vertices span at most $m-1$ edges of the tree. Maximizing the tree weight gives the strongest such bound. At $m=0$ the point contributes one survivor. Pair intersection sizes can be computed without visiting all numerators. [Perarnau and Serra, Lemma 13](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/29405874/Perarnau_Correlation_among_runners_Journal_Combinatorics.pdf) · [Pair-sum characterization, Kravitz, Proposition 2.1](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3wx931fh/qt3wx931fh.pdf#page=5)

For speeds $c,e$ on denominator $d$ set $g=\gcd(c,e,d)$, $D=d/g$, $u=c/g$, $v=e/g$, $s=\gcd(u,D)$, $H=D/s$. Define $A=\lfloor(H-1)/n\rfloor$, $B=\lfloor(D-1)/n\rfloor$ and $K=v(u/s)^{-1}\bmod H$, taking $K=0$ for $H=1$. Then
$$
|B_c\cap B_e|=
g\sum_{a=-A}^A
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{B-Ka}{H}\right\rfloor-
      \left\lfloor\frac{-B-1-Ka}{H}\right\rfloor\right).
$$
After removing the common factor $g$, the first blocking test fixes a signed residual $a$ modulo $H$. Each such residual has $s$ lifts. Since $\gcd(v,s)=1$, their second residuals run once through the residues modulo $D$ congruent to $Ka$ modulo $H$. The floors count which signed representatives fall in $[-B,B]$. The Euclidean floor-sum algorithm evaluates the two sums in $O(\log d)$ arithmetic steps for a fixed pair. This addresses pair-size computation, not the cost of finding the witness itself. Our replay compares against enumerated block sets on 20,828 unordered folded-class pairs across the two prescribed thresholds, in both orders and including zero classes. [Implementation](pair_overlap.py)

**Information limit.** On $d=16$, compare $(1,3,8,13)$ with $(1,7,8,15)$. Their three signed classes have the same size list $(7,7,8)$, and all three pair intersections have size 3 in each case. The triple intersections have sizes 1 and 3 respectively. Exactly two numerators, 7 and 9, survive in the first tuple. None survive in the second. Thus even exact singleton and pair counts on one grid can lose the answer. Class labels and the original speeds still allow recovery. The example also prevents treating a zero tree bound as a certificate of absence.

## Why counting a fixed number of overlaps can fail

For every $q\ge1$, let $V=(1,2,4,30q)$ and $n=5$. The internal pair grids $3,5,6$ are blocked everywhere by $30q$. On each remaining grid $d=30q+c_0$ with $c_0$ one of $1,2,4$, the fast runner folds onto $c_0$. Exactly three signed classes remain. Each has size at least $2d/5-\gcd(c,d)$, and their gcds sum to at most $1+2+4=7$. Crediting duplication at $K$ named numerators, however cleverly selected, can reclaim at most $2K$. A positive count from this rule requires
$$
K>d/10-7/2.
$$
For $q\ge3$ this excludes the three-residue rule on every grid. For $q=1,2$ the untruncated three-residue bounds on the three crossing grids are respectively $(-2,-1,-1)$ and $(-8,-9,-9)$, also inconclusive. The obstruction concerns a count obtained solely by crediting individually inspected duplicates.

Yet throughout $[3/10,2/5]$ all three core speeds are safe. At $a=\lceil3d/10\rceil$ the time $a/d$ lies in that interval for every crossing grid here, since $d\ge31$. This constructs a witness in one step. For $V=(1,2,4,30)$ and $d=31$, it gives $10/31$ with distances $(10,11,9,10)/31$. More generally, an interval safe for a core transfers to any grid $d=r+c$ with $c$ in that core whenever its length is at least $1/d$. This was the starting point for asking what survives after *arbitrarily many* additional dyadic constraints. [Sharp result](DYADIC_EXTENSION.md)

**Return to Representation Science.** Signed residues preserve each labeled runner's distance on a fixed grid. A lower count preserves less. Retaining the fact that an inspected point had zero blockers can change a tuple outcome at no extra probing cost. Even all same-grid pair counts can merge two cases that disagree on existence. A witness requires the actual numerator or a constructive rule that supplies it.

**Next.** For the broader grid problem, seek an arithmetic condition for choosing a crossing denominator with a positive bound, or a recovery rule for its numerator, before seeing the full scan. For the dyadic class, test whether two added speeds always permit $1/6$. The pair $(3,5)$ forces this ceiling and already defeats the particular witness family used in the one-extra proof.
